One of my favorite poetic forms is the sonnet.

If you've forgotten what exactly a sonnet is, save perhaps for some vague notion of Shakespeare, allow me to explain. The Shakespearean sonnet (the other common type, if you're curious, is the Italian, or Petrarchan, sonnet, but that's besides the point) follows 4 quatrains and a couplet. In simpler terms, a sonnet uses the following pattern:

a
b
a
b
c
d
c
d
e
f
e
f
g
g

So the first line rhymes with the third line, the second line rhymes with the fourth line, and so on until you get to the last two lines, which rhyme with each other.

Not only does a sonnet have to follow rhyming rules, but it also has to follow a specific flow, or meter. For sonnets, that meter is iambic pentameter, which basically means that each line has ten syllables, with the emphasis on every other syllable. (Shall **I** com**PARE** thee **TO** a **SUM**mers **DAY**...)

But here's the thing. The best sonnets? The ones that really capture your attention? They bend the rules. Sometimes they break them. But you can't just break the rules randomly or thoughtlessly. You have to know the rules inside and out, so when you break them, it's for a reason.

In the last part of the book, I talked about six characteristics of quality. But blindly adhering to those characteristics would be too constrictive. The real world is far messier. Deadlines shift. Features are added or dropped. User journeys veer into unexpected directions.

To really focus on quality, you have to not only know the rules, you have to know it's okay to break them. But, like adding an extra syllable to a sonnet, you have to be thoughtful about it.

In the following chapters, we'll talk about how to apply these characteristics, so you can decide not just how to follow the rules, but when to break them.
